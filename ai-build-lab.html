Where this starts

Two situations where this applies

Almost every engagement starts in one of these two places.

The manual situation

Spreadsheets and email attachments

Work that runs on someone remembering to send the update. It functions, until it stops scaling, and nobody can see what is happening without asking for it.

The wrong-fit situation

A platform built for a different business

A monthly subscription for something designed around a different kind of company. It half fits. Everyone works around it. The workarounds have quietly become the process.

What could we build today that was not economically practical two years ago?

AI-assisted development and modern infrastructure collapsed the cost of creating software, automations, dashboards and customer experiences. Systems that used to require a development team and a six-figure budget now take weeks. AI Build Lab exists to act on that.

The engagement

How a build sprint works

Strategy, design, build and handover, one engagement, scoped to a single opportunity.

  1. Opportunity session

    We work through where value is actually being lost, revenue, customer experience, or time spent on work that should not still be manual, and pick the one opportunity worth building first.

  2. Solution design

    We define what should exist: what it does, who uses it, what it connects to, and what it deliberately will not do. Scoped small enough to ship fast and prove value.

  3. Build

    The system gets built and integrated with the tools you already run, with working versions in your hands early, rather than a reveal at the end.

  4. Launch and handover

    Live, in use, and yours. You own the system, the data and the infrastructure. There is no platform to keep renting from us.

  5. Expand from there

    One working system usually reveals the next one. Engagements continue because something proved out, not because a contract required it.

Questions

AI Build Lab, answered

What is an AI build lab?

An AI build lab is a focused engagement that designs and builds one working system for your business, a client portal, a member platform, a buyer-facing application, or an automation between tools that do not talk. AI is used hands-on to design, write and debug the build, which is what makes weeks possible instead of quarters.

The important word is one. The failure mode of most internal software efforts is scope: a platform that will eventually do everything, shipped never.

Scoping to a single opportunity, with an explicit list of what it deliberately will not do, is what keeps a build sprint inside two or three months.

The VGL perspective

The interesting question is not what AI can build. It is what is now worth building that was not worth building two years ago.

What can actually be built in 60 days?

A full community and member platform with sign-up, login, subscription billing, live content and member data went live in about 60 days. Client portals with dashboards, documents and automatic status updates land in a similar window. Automations between existing systems are usually faster, often the lowest-risk place to start.

Sixty days is not a promise attached to every build. It is what one real community platform took, delivered for less than the client had been paying to rent a platform that fit them poorly.

What determines the timeline is scope discipline and how many existing systems the build has to integrate with, not raw build speed.

The VGL perspective

Sixty days is what one real platform took. We would rather quote the instance than imply the rule.

Do we own the code and infrastructure?

Yes. You own the system, the data and the infrastructure outright. There is no platform to keep renting from us and no per-seat fee after launch. That is the core difference from adopting another subscription product: when the engagement ends, the system keeps running and it belongs to you.

Ownership changes the economics over a three-year horizon. A subscription that half fits costs you every month indefinitely and still leaves the workarounds in place.

It also changes the incentive on our side. We cannot keep you through switching costs, so engagements continue only when something proved out.

The VGL perspective

Every alternative except a build you own is a recurring cost. That sentence is the whole argument.

How is this different from hiring a development agency?

An agency builds what you specify, on a six-figure budget over two quarters, and usually keeps the maintenance relationship. Build Lab starts with the business opportunity rather than a specification, scopes to one system small enough to ship fast, and hands over ownership at launch. Strategy, design and build sit in one engagement.

The specification gap is the expensive part. An agency needs you to already know what to build, which means the strategy work happens before the engagement, usually informally and usually incompletely.

Starting at the opportunity means the first session is about where value is leaking, not about requirements gathering for something already decided.

The VGL perspective

Most companies do not need an AI department. They need someone who can decide what to build, and then build it.

Engagements run $2,000 to $5,000 per month across a two to three month focused build sprint. That covers strategy, design, build and automation together. There is no AI department or development team to hire, and the engagement is scoped to one opportunity at a time rather than a six-figure transformation project.

At the top of that range, a three-month sprint totals around $15,000 for a system you own permanently. The agency comparison for equivalent scope typically starts at six figures.

What should we build first?

Start with the smallest system that proves value: one automation, one dashboard, or one workflow that should not still be manual. Automation and integration work is usually the lowest-risk entry point. The opportunity session exists to find that first build, and one working system reliably reveals the next one.

Starting small is not a hedge against ambition. It is how you find out whether the thing you assumed was the bottleneck actually is.

Teams consistently discover the second build is more valuable than the first, and they only discover it by shipping the first.

The VGL perspective

Every build we have started small produced a second engagement. Every one that started large spent its first month arguing about scope.
